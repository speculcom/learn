Robot software and learning route

BuilderKnow the basics and start building

about 45 minutes

First separate policy, demonstration and simulation, then look at middleware, planning and transfer. The goal is to understand the software and learning layers behind robot demos, without treating teleoperation or simulation success as real autonomous capability.

Checkpoint
  1. Explain how policy, imitation learning and reinforcement learning relate, and where teleoperation data usually enters training.
  2. Explain why passing in simulation does not mean passing on hardware, and name at least one way to narrow the reality gap.
  3. When watching a robot demo, list three questions: human-controlled or autonomous, simulation or hardware, and what the training data covers.
  4. Why should imitation-learning data cover failures and recoveries?
  5. What conditions should teleoperation data record before training?
  6. What three reality gaps are most often missed in physics simulation?
  7. What is the difference between motion planning and a policy?
  8. What does robot middleware mainly solve?
  9. In a VLA model, what do vision, language and action each refer to?
  10. On seeing a “90% success rate” robot result, what three things do you ask first?

Try answering before opening the reference. Revisit the relevant step above if needed. These answers highlight key ideas; you do not need to repeat the wording.

Question 1 · Reference answer

A policy maps observations to actions; imitation learning fits a policy from demonstrations, while reinforcement learning optimises it by reward. Teleoperated demonstrations usually become imitation-learning data.

Question 2 · Reference answer

Simulation differs from reality in friction, delay, sensor noise and contact models. Domain randomisation, dynamics randomisation or system identification can narrow the gap, but results must be rechecked on hardware.

Question 3 · Reference answer

First ask whether actions are human teleoperation or autonomous model execution; then whether validation was in simulation or on hardware; finally what tasks and scenes the training data covers and who handles uncovered cases.

Question 4 · Reference answer

Success-only demonstrations leave the policy unable to return after deviation; failure and human-recovery samples teach correction. Without recovery data, one drift stops the robot.

Revisit this concept

Question 5 · Reference answer

Record operator, hardware, camera pose, latency, gripper/force settings, success criteria and intervention points. Without them data cannot be reproduced, nor whether the policy learned human compensation or the task.

Revisit this concept

Question 6 · Reference answer

Contact friction and compliance, sensor noise and latency, actuator saturation and backlash. All can destabilise a simulated policy on hardware; randomise or identify, then recheck on the real machine.

Revisit this concept

Question 7 · Reference answer

Planning searches a feasible trajectory under known maps and constraints; a policy maps observations to actions under uncertainty and contact. They can chain, but planning success does not mean the policy handles disturbance.

Revisit this concept

Question 8 · Reference answer

It standardises messaging, time sync, discovery and lifecycle between nodes, decoupling perception, planning and control. It does not guarantee real-time behaviour; scheduling and QoS still depend on hardware and task.

Revisit this concept

Question 9 · Reference answer

Vision is camera/depth observation, language is the task instruction or scene description, action is end-effector trajectory, joint targets or discrete skills. A VLA ties them into one policy; it does not automatically yield hardware generalisation.

Revisit this concept

Question 10 · Reference answer

Ask the test distribution (objects, scenes, initial poses), the success criterion (who judges, retries allowed), and simulation or hardware. If any differs, 90% is not comparable.
