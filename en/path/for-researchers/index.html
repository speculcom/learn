Checkpoint
  1. Split a tech report's training section into pretraining and post-training, naming the key variables of each
  2. On seeing a "new SOTA" claim, first check the benchmark's release date against the model's training cutoff
  3. When reading a model card, point out the license, the eval setup, and the stated limitations
  4. For each paper card on this route, state its research question and where the core evidence sits, and name at least one popular claim the paper never makes
  5. When reading a paper, what should you take from the abstract, setup and limitations?
  6. Does lower perplexity mean better generation quality?
  7. How do knowledge distillation and quantisation differ in goal?
  8. Why does the evidence location on a paper card matter more than the conclusion?
  9. Why distinguish open weights from open-source models, and why read the licence?
  10. When reading a model card, how do you judge whether its evaluation is reproducible?

Try answering before opening the reference. Revisit the relevant step above if needed. These answers highlight key ideas; you do not need to repeat the wording.

Question 1 · Reference answer

Separate base training, supervised data and preference optimization. Record data sources, objectives and evaluation setup; leave undisclosed recipe details unknown.

Revisit this concept

Question 2 · Reference answer

Compare dataset release, training cutoff, benchmark version and decontamination. Temporal overlap suggests risk, not proof of contamination; inspect checks and held-out tasks.

Revisit this concept

Question 3 · Reference answer

Separate experimentally supported, setup-limited and undisclosed claims. English-only model-card results do not establish Chinese performance or production reliability.

Revisit this concept

Question 4 · Reference answer

For LIMA, inspect controlled human evaluation and ablations on small high-quality instruction datasets. This does not establish that little data solves every safety or robustness problem. Apply the same question/evidence/limits structure to other papers.

Revisit this concept

Question 5 · Reference answer

The abstract gives the question and main claim; the setup gives data, baselines, compute and evaluation; limitations give the scope and gaps the authors admit. None substitutes for another.

Revisit this concept

Question 6 · Reference answer

Perplexity measures how well a model predicts text; it is not human preference, factual correctness or task success. It compares language modelling under the same setup, not product quality on its own.

Revisit this concept

Question 7 · Reference answer

Distillation trains a smaller model on a larger model’s outputs or representations to transfer capability; quantisation lowers weight/activation precision to save memory and bandwidth. They can combine, with different failure modes.

Revisit this concept

Question 8 · Reference answer

Conclusions get simplified in abstracts or media; the evidence location leads back to the exact table, figure and setup, showing whether data supports the claim. A citation without a location is just retelling.

Revisit this concept

Question 9 · Reference answer

Open weights only means the weights can be downloaded; commercial use, redistribution or derivatives may be restricted. Open source also covers code, training data and licence terms. The licence decides whether weights can ship in a product.

Revisit this concept

Question 10 · Reference answer

Check whether it gives model version, prompts and system settings, sample set and split, method, judges and dates. Missing any one makes the score a reference, not reproducible evidence.
